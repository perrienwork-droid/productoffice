Theory

What marketing copywriting is and why words drive revenue

Copywriting is writing that moves people to action. Not writing that informs (that's content), not writing that entertains (that's creative writing), not writing that documents (that's technical writing) — writing that persuades someone to click, sign up, buy, or believe. Every marketing surface runs on copy: headlines, CTAs, email subject lines, ad text, landing page bodies, product descriptions, pricing page explanations. The difference between copy that converts and copy that doesn't is often the difference between a profitable campaign and a money-losing one.

Storytelling is copywriting's strategic layer. Where copywriting techniques optimize individual sentences and phrases, storytelling creates the larger narrative arc that gives those sentences meaning. A brand without a story is just a collection of features and prices. A brand with a compelling story — how it started, what it believes, who it serves, what future it's building — gives customers a reason to care beyond the product itself. Story creates emotional connection; copy converts that connection into action.

The distinction between marketing copywriting and UX writing is important. UX writing (covered in UX.2.10) serves the user inside the product — interface labels, error messages, onboarding copy. Marketing copywriting serves the prospect outside the product — ads, emails, landing pages, sales materials. UX writing optimizes for clarity and task completion. Marketing copywriting optimizes for persuasion and conversion. Both require economy of language, but the goals diverge.

Practical

Copywriting formulas

PAS (Problem–Agitate–Solve) Core Method

Use when: writing sales pages, email sequences, or any long-form copy where you need to build urgency before presenting the solution. PAS is the most versatile copywriting formula — it works for everything from a 3-sentence ad to a 2,000-word sales page.

Problem. State the problem the reader is experiencing. Be specific — vague problems don't resonate. Not "Managing projects is hard" but "You spend more time updating status spreadsheets than doing actual work." The reader should nod: "Yes, that's exactly my situation."

Agitate. Make the problem feel urgent. Describe the consequences of not solving it: wasted time, lost revenue, frustrated teams, missed deadlines. Agitation isn't about creating fear — it's about making the reader feel the full weight of a problem they've been tolerating. "Every week you spend 5 hours on status updates is 5 hours not spent shipping. Over a quarter, that's 65 hours — more than a week and a half of engineering time — burned on spreadsheet maintenance."

Solve. Present your product as the resolution. The transition from agitation to solution should feel like relief: "What if your project status updated itself?" The solution section should match the specificity of the problem. If you described a concrete pain, offer a concrete resolution — not vague promises.

AIDA (Attention–Interest–Desire–Action) Core Method

Use when: writing landing pages, ads, or any copy where you need to move someone from unaware to action in a single piece. AIDA maps the psychological journey from interruption to conversion.

Attention. Stop the scroll. The headline, the first line, the image — whatever the reader encounters first must interrupt their current mental state. Surprising statistics, provocative questions, bold claims, or pattern interruptions work. "Your marketing team is spending $47,000/year on tools nobody uses" stops a CMO faster than "Introducing a better marketing stack."

Interest. Now that you have attention, earn the right to keep it. Provide information that's relevant to the reader's situation. Expand on the attention-grabbing hook with specifics: "We audited 200 marketing teams and found that the average team uses 12 tools but relies on only 4. The rest are shelfware — paid for annually, used never."

Desire. Shift from information to aspiration. Show the reader what their life looks like with your solution. Testimonials, before/after comparisons, demos, and outcome descriptions build desire. "Teams that consolidated to our platform saved an average of $38,000/year and got a single source of truth for campaign performance."

Action. Tell the reader exactly what to do next. One clear CTA, not three. "Start your free audit" is better than offering a free trial, a demo, and a white paper simultaneously. Reduce friction: if the CTA requires a form, keep it short. If it requires a meeting, let them book it instantly.

Before–After–Bridge Core Method

Use when: writing shorter copy — email subject lines + body, social ads, product descriptions — where you need to communicate transformation concisely.

Before: Paint the current state (the world without your product). After: Paint the desired state (the world with your product). Bridge: Connect the two — your product is the bridge from before to after. Example: "Before: You spend Friday afternoons manually building reports. After: Reports generate themselves overnight, ready in your inbox Monday morning. Bridge: [Product] connects your data sources and delivers the reports you need — automatically." The power of this formula is its simplicity: it forces you to articulate the transformation in concrete terms.

Headline and CTA craft

Headline Formula Library Core Method

Use when: writing headlines for landing pages, blog posts, ads, or email subject lines. Headlines do 80% of the work — most people read the headline and decide whether to continue. A brilliant body with a weak headline is invisible.

The "How to" headline. "How to [achieve desired outcome] without [common pain]." Works because it promises a specific result while removing a feared obstacle. "How to double your email open rates without writing clickbait."

The number headline. "7 [things] that [outcome]." Numbers set expectations for scope and effort. Odd numbers slightly outperform even numbers in testing. Specific numbers outperform round numbers: "7" beats "10" because it feels researched rather than arbitrary.

The question headline. "Are you still [outdated practice]?" Questions create an open cognitive loop — the reader needs to keep reading to close it. Works best when the reader likely answers "yes" and feels mildly uncomfortable about it.

The proof headline. "How [Company] achieved [specific result] in [timeframe]." Combines social proof with specificity. "How Stripe processes $1 trillion in payments" is more compelling than "A better payment platform."

The contrast headline. "[Common belief] is wrong. Here's why." Challenges assumptions and creates curiosity. "A/B testing is making your product worse" stops a product manager mid-scroll because it challenges something they believe in.

CTA Writing Guidelines Core Method

Use when: writing call-to-action buttons, links, or instructions. The CTA is the moment of conversion — the difference between a reader and a customer.

Use first person. "Start my free trial" outperforms "Start your free trial" in most tests. First person creates ownership before the click.

Emphasize value, not action. "Get my free report" outperforms "Download now." The reader cares about what they'll get, not the mechanical action of downloading. "See my savings" beats "Calculate" because it focuses on the outcome.

Reduce perceived risk. "Start free — no credit card required" addresses the objection before it forms. If the CTA asks for commitment (a demo, a meeting), reduce friction: "Book a 15-minute call" is less intimidating than "Request a demo."

One CTA per context. A landing page with "Start free trial," "Watch demo," "Read case study," and "Talk to sales" converts worse than one with a single, clear primary CTA. If you must offer secondary options, visually subordinate them so the primary CTA dominates.

Brand storytelling

Storytelling Arc for Brands Core Method

Use when: crafting the brand narrative — the overarching story that gives meaning to your marketing, guides your content, and helps customers connect emotionally with your company.

The hero is the customer, not the brand. The most common brand storytelling mistake is making the company the hero of its own story. In effective brand narrative, the customer is the hero — they have a problem, they're on a journey, and your brand is the guide that helps them succeed. Your "About" page shouldn't read like a résumé of company achievements; it should read like a declaration of what you believe about your customer's world and how you help them navigate it.

The origin story. Why does this company exist? The best origin stories connect the founder's personal frustration to the customer's ongoing pain. "We built this because we experienced the same problem and couldn't find a good solution" creates authenticity and shared identity. The origin story doesn't need to be dramatic — it needs to be genuine and relevant to the customer's situation.

The enemy. Every compelling narrative has an antagonist. In brand storytelling, the enemy isn't a competitor — it's the status quo, the broken process, the outdated way of doing things. "We're fighting the complexity that makes software hard to use" gives the brand purpose beyond profit. When customers share the enemy, they join a movement rather than making a purchase.

The vision. Where is this going? What future is the brand building? Vision stories create aspiration: "We believe everyone should have access to professional-grade design tools" (Canva) or "We're building a world where work happens automatically" (Zapier). The vision gives meaning to the day-to-day product and helps customers see themselves as part of something larger.

Tone Adaptation by Channel Core Method

Use when: maintaining consistent brand voice across channels while adapting tone and format to each channel's expectations.

Voice stays constant — it's who you are. Tone adapts to context — it's how you sound in different situations. A brand that's witty and direct might express that as: sharp one-liners on social, conversational but structured in email, authoritative with personality in blog posts, concise and action-oriented in ads, and warm and encouraging in onboarding flows. The personality is recognizable across channels; the delivery is appropriate to each.

Email: Subject lines under 50 characters. First line visible in preview earns the open. Conversational tone, short paragraphs, one primary CTA. Social: Platform-native format. LinkedIn tolerates longer form; Twitter rewards compression; Instagram demands visual-first. Landing pages: Benefit-led headlines, scannable structure, proof points, single CTA. Ads: Maximum compression — every word must earn its space. Lead with the differentiation or the curiosity hook, not the brand name.

Examples

Copywriting and storytelling in practice

Pattern: The headline rewrite that doubled conversion

A SaaS landing page originally headlined "The Complete HR Platform for Modern Teams." It described the product accurately but created no urgency or curiosity. The team tested a PAS-style rewrite: "Your HR team wastes 12 hours a week on manual processes. Here's how to get those hours back." Conversion rate (visitor to free trial) increased from 2.1% to 4.4%. The original headline told; the rewrite sold. It named a specific, relatable pain, quantified the cost, and promised a concrete benefit. The product behind the headline was identical — only the words changed.

Pattern: The brand story that drove organic sharing

A fintech company targeting freelancers built their brand story around the shared enemy: traditional banking that doesn't understand independent workers. Their About page opened with "We were freelancers who got tired of banks treating us like we didn't have real jobs." Their content consistently framed freelancers as the heroes of the modern economy, with the company as the tool that gave them the financial infrastructure they deserved. This narrative resonated so deeply that customers shared the company's content not because of features, but because it expressed something they felt about their own identity. Organic referral became their largest acquisition channel — people don't share product specs, but they share stories they see themselves in.

Pattern: The CTA test that revealed what customers actually wanted

A B2B product tested three CTA variants on their homepage: "Start free trial" (action-focused), "See how it works" (education-focused), and "See what you're missing" (curiosity + loss aversion). "See what you're missing" won by 41% over the free trial CTA. The insight: prospects at the homepage stage weren't ready to commit to a trial — they wanted to understand the gap between their current state and what was possible. The winning CTA met them at their actual stage of readiness rather than rushing to the conversion the company wanted.

Common pitfalls

⚠

Writing for yourself instead of the customer. Copy reviewed by internal stakeholders gets optimized for what the company wants to say, not what the customer needs to hear. "Our proprietary AI engine leverages state-of-the-art NLP" impresses nobody outside the engineering team. Write from the customer's vocabulary, about the customer's problems, for the customer's benefit.

⚠

Clever over clear. Puns, wordplay, and creative headlines feel satisfying to write and award-winning in portfolios. They also confuse readers who don't share your cultural context, skim rather than read, or encounter your copy in translation. Clarity converts more reliably than cleverness. Be clear first; be clever only if clarity is preserved.

⚠

Ignoring the read-to-action gap. Beautiful copy that doesn't lead anywhere wastes the reader's engagement. Every piece of marketing copy should end with a clear, specific, low-friction next step. If the reader finishes and thinks "That was interesting" but doesn't know what to do next, the copy failed — no matter how well-written it was.

⚠

Formula dependence. PAS, AIDA, and BAB are scaffolding — structures to build on, not templates to fill in mechanically. Copy that reads like a formula feels formulaic. Internalize the logic behind the formulas (identify pain → create urgency → present solution → drive action), then write naturally within that logic.

Further reading and resources

Essential reading

"The Copywriter's Handbook" by Robert Bly — Comprehensive copywriting reference covering every format and channel, with practical techniques that have stood the test of time.

"Building a StoryBrand" by Donald Miller — Frameworks for brand storytelling that place the customer as the hero and the brand as the guide.

"Everybody Writes" by Ann Handley — Modern writing guide for marketers covering content, copy, and the discipline of writing well in a digital context.

"Ogilvy on Advertising" by David Ogilvy — Classic advertising principles from the field's most famous practitioner, still relevant for headline writing, long-form copy, and brand voice.
